Field colour

The subject is drawn on a flat field and that field is cut away afterwards. Green is the film industry's default because it sits far from skin and warm tones; pink sits far from foliage. Neither wins on every subject, so both are here and both were measured.

What to do with the picture

Cut it out keeps your photograph and removes its background. No GPU is involved at all. Re-draw it hands the picture to the image engine as a starting point and returns illustration — a new image seeded by yours, not a copy of it.

Higher means the engine keeps less of what you sent. The slider stops at 0.80 and that ceiling was measured, not chosen for feel: at 0.85 two completely different uploads — a cat and a guitar — produced byte-identical stickers. Past that point your picture is not being used, and a control that pretends otherwise would be lying about what it does.

Parameters

512 px square. The engine is distilled for four steps; more costs time and buys little, but the ceiling is yours to find.

 

Three ways in, and what each one really does

They are not three features bolted to one engine. They are three different amounts of work, and two of them do not touch the GPU at all — which is the whole reason they are described separately rather than behind one button that hides the difference.

Type it Your words, plus the suffix below, go to the image engine. The subject is drawn on a flat field and that field is cut away here on the CPU. This is the path the sixteen-render trial further down measured.
Upload a picture — cut it out measured 1.17–1.21 s to segment, median 1.2 s, plus 0.01 s to encode, over 4 runs. No GPU whatsoever — this path never asks for the card, so it runs while the card is busy rendering for somebody else. Your photograph comes back, its background gone; nothing is generated and nothing is invented.
Upload a picture — re-draw it measured 11.64–12.17 s on the card, median 11.82 s over 4 runs at strength 0.60. Your picture seeds a new image; the result is illustration, not your photograph, and the page says so on every sticker it returns.
Say it measured 0.73–0.9 s to transcribe 11.0 s of speech, median 0.77 s over 4 runs, then the typed path exactly as above. This is speech → text → the same render a typed subject gets. Nothing here listens to audio and draws from it; calling it “audio to sticker” without that sentence would claim a capability this box does not have. The transcript comes back with the sticker so you can see which of the two got it wrong.

Transcription runs on the CPU with vendor ggml-base.en, English, and never takes the GPU lock — so the words are already turned into a subject by the time the card is asked for. Up to 30 seconds is used and the rest is discarded rather than silently truncated mid-sentence without saying so.

Why the re-draw slider stops at 0.80

Because past that the upload stops mattering, and a control that keeps moving after it has stopped doing anything is a lie about what the machine is doing.

measured At strength 0.85 two completely different uploads — a photograph of a cat and a photograph of a guitar — produced stickers with identical SHA-256 hashes. Not similar: the same file. At that setting the engine has discarded the input entirely and is drawing from the prompt alone, so “image to sticker” would have been describing something that was not happening. The ceiling is 0.80 and the API refuses anything above it rather than quietly clamping, because a request that did not do what it asked for should say so.

A related surprise, kept here rather than smoothed over: the engine quantises strength into whole denoise steps, so at four steps 0.55 and 0.70 are the same setting. The slider is therefore coarser than it looks, and that is a property of the engine rather than of this page.

Why a photograph gets a different cut from a generated sticker

Key what we painted, segment what we did not. When this surface draws the sticker it also chooses the background, so the colour key described further down is exact and costs about 0.12 s. When you supply the picture, nobody chose the background, and a colour-distance threshold cannot tell a white chest from a light grey backdrop.

It did not, measurably. Keying a photograph of a tabby against a studio backdrop returned measured 88.75% transparent and reported success by every number the surface had — while punching holes through the cat's chest fur, one ear and a front leg. The numbers all said it worked. The sticker was ruined. That is exactly the case a threshold cannot detect, which is why the decision is made by what the picture is rather than by a quality score.

Uploaded pictures therefore go to a segmentation model instead (measured 1.17–1.21 s, CPU, in an isolated environment that never takes the GPU lock). Which matte actually ran is reported on every sticker rather than assumed from the mode — on a box where the model is not installed the cut falls back to the colour key, and the response says colour key, because the sticker really is worse and hiding that would make the reading useless.

The first cut after a restart is slow and the figure is published anyway. measured 32.9 s, once, immediately after the API came up with a cold page cache — against measured 1.17–1.21 s thereafter. Every request starts a fresh process and re-reads a 168 MB model, so the fast number holds only once the operating system has that file cached. Quoting 1.2 s on its own would be quoting a warm cache as if it were a guarantee.

One useful consequence of no GPU being involved: the cut is measured deterministic. The same photograph returned the same 188 KiB file with 77.9% transparency on all 4 runs, byte for byte. The generated paths cannot promise that, and do not.

What gets added to what you type

This surface rewrites your prompt, so it shows you the rewrite. Your subject goes in front; this goes on the end of every request, and it is the whole reason the background can be removed at all — asking for “no background” produces nothing useful, because the engine has no concept of absence and will draw something. Naming a flat colour gives the cut something to find.

What this actually costs, on this machine

Render, 512 px, four steps measured 9.5–11.3 s of engine wall-clock across 16 renders on 2026-08-17, median 9.6 s, waiting for the card excluded. Not scaled from the 768 px figure elsewhere on this site — step count, attention and tiling do not scale with area.
Cutting the background out measured 0.1–0.16 s on the CPU, median 0.12 s, for the whole post-render step: decode, key, despill, feather and re-encode with an alpha channel. It runs after the GPU lock is handed back, so it costs the next surface in the queue nothing.
Sticker file size measured 208–371 KiB across the same 16 renders, median 272 KiB, against vendor a 500 KB per-sticker cap in Apple's Human Interface Guidelines for Messages. Every one of the sixteen came in under it, which is a result on these sixteen and not a guarantee about the next one.
How much of the frame ends up transparent measured 56–92% of pixels. The figure comes back with every sticker, because a cut that failed produces a file that decodes perfectly and is a solid square — and a number is the only thing that tells those apart without looking.
Did the cut work? measured 8/8 on the pink field and 8/8 on the green, over eight subject types — animal, object, person, dark, light, spindly, text-heavy and warm-coloured — at fixed seeds, each rendered once against each field so a failure could be blamed on the right thing. Sixteen renders is a small sample and is quoted as one.

measured means taken on this box, on the date stated. vendor means read off a publisher's specification. Nothing on this page is a projection, and nothing here is a price: pricing questions go to symonsayadmin@symoneural.com.

How the transparency is made, and the two things that went wrong first

The engine returns three channels and has no transparency output of any kind, so the fourth channel is made here: the subject is rendered on a flat field, the field is measured, and every region of that colour connected to the frame edge is made transparent. The connection test is what stops a hole being punched through the subject when it legitimately contains the field colour — an eye, a highlight, a gap between an arm and a body. Edge pixels that picked up the field are corrected before the transparency is attached, because otherwise the sticker wears a coloured halo the moment it lands on a white message bubble.

The field colour is measured, not assumed. The prompt asks for a flat #FF00FF. Asked for exactly that, the engine painted (243, 1, 98) to (252, 67, 114) — a hot pink — consistently, on every subject. Cutting against the colour that was requested matched nothing at all: the first sixteen renders came back 0% transparent, which is a file that decodes perfectly and is worthless. The cut now reads the field off the render's own border, and reports what it found with every sticker.

The GPU lock could not be taken the recommended way. This box hands the card to one surface at a time through a governor, and the recommended form wraps the job so the card is returned even on a crash. That form kills this particular job: making room for a new tenant works by matching the previous tenant's command line, and the wrapper's own command line contains the engine's name, so the governor terminated the process that was running the governor — silently, sixteen times, while logging a successful handover. The lock is now taken and released explicitly, always naming this surface, and always returned even when a render fails.

What it still gets wrong. The connection test cannot tell a hole in the subject from detail inside it, so background trapped inside a shape — the middle of a coiled cable, the gap inside a handle — stays opaque and comes back as a coloured patch. That is the deliberate trade: the alternative cuts a hole through every eye and every highlight. It showed up on 2 of the 16 test renders, both of them the same coiled-cable subject, and the fix today is to pick a subject without an enclosed gap.

Both of those are on this page rather than quietly fixed because negative results are the ones worth publishing: they are what the next person needs and what a brochure would leave out.

What a sticker file carries, and how long the link lasts

Homepage art on this site carries a printed bar naming the model, size and seed. A sticker does not: a caption strip burned into the artwork is not a sticker. The same facts ride inside the PNG's metadata instead — software, model, seed, steps, canvas, field colour and the full prompt — so a sticker saved to a phone still knows how it was made and not one pixel of it is defaced.

The link is temporary and the response says so. Renders on this box are kept to the newest sixty and then deleted, because a public endpoint writing a few hundred kilobytes per request fills a disk quietly and takes the whole site down with it. Keep the file, not the URL.

System information — one card, one surface at a time

This is a 16 GB consumer card, and it is a test bench

Chat and image never run at the same time here. A lock hands the GPU to exactly one surface and the other waits, because both weight sets do not fit in 15.92 GiB at once — and when they swap, we wait for the driver to actually hand the memory back before the next one starts. If you are queued behind someone, that is not a bug; it is one card doing one thing at a time.

RavenCalc is the exception — it is symbolic algebra on the CPU, so it never touches the GPU and never queues. Everything here is a demonstration of the stack on the smallest machine we would ever ship, not a statement of capacity. A server we spec for you would not be sharing one GPU with itself.
